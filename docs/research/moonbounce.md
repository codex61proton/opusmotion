## 0. Read this first — corrections to the brief

1. **Frank Eliot's "Moon Bounce ELINT" is a CIA article, not an NSA Technical Journal article.** It is *Studies in Intelligence* vol. 11 no. 2 (Spring 1967), printed pp. 59–65, classified SECRET/No Foreign Dissem, released 1994 (CIA Historical Review Program). The title is "Moon Bounce ELINT" (three words).
2. **N. C. Gerson's "SIGINT in Space" is also a *Studies in Intelligence* article** (vol. 28 no. 2, Summer 1984, pp. 41–48, SECRET). Gerson was an NSA scientist. He published other pieces in the NSA Technical Journal, but not this one.
3. **The NSA Technical Journal does contain a moon-reflection article.** The cumulative index lists "Extending the Radio Horizon for Intercept Purposes by Using the Moon as a Reflector (C)", vol. X no. 2, Spring 1965. The author is redacted in the index. I could not find the article text among NSA's released PDFs (nsa.gov also returned HTTP 403 to the sandbox's curl for other NSA PDFs). Do not attribute it to Gerson or anyone else.
4. **The CIA website's retyped PDF of Eliot has a transcription error.** It says the Moon technique "emerged in 1948". The original typeset page (printed p. 59, checked visually) says **1946**, which matches Project Diana (10 Jan 1946). Use 1946. Both copies are saved locally.
5. **Several different "Moon" programmes exist and must not be merged.** They are (a) the Navy's PAMOR / Sugar Grove line (1948–1962), (b) the Navy/CIA Hen House intercepts (1964–67), (c) NSA's Arecibo experiment, and (d) Poteat's memoir about the Tall King radars, which used a 60-ft dish at Moorestown, NJ. Poteat's separate MELODY project used reflections off Soviet *missiles*, not the Moon.

## B. Verified facts

Format: fact. “quote” [source, printed page]. Anything not in quotation marks is my paraphrase.

### B1. The problem the Moon solved
- Soviet radars deep inside the USSR could not be heard from the ground. “all radars operate on wave lengths which are too short to be reflected back to earth by the ionosphere but pass through and are generally lost in outer space. Under ordinary conditions, therefore, we are unable to observe them from ground collection sites when they are more than a few hundred miles inside the denied area of the Soviet Union.” [S1a p. 59; Eliot says “Except for a very few specialized over-the-horizon devices”]
- Why the intelligence mattered: “We need to know the parameters of these radars as accurately as possible in order to plan penetration strategy and design the most effective equipment for electronic countermeasures.” [S1a p. 59]
- “Manned overflights of the Soviet Union are no longer permitted.” [S1a p. 59]

### B2. Origin of the Moon technique
- The first radar echo off the Moon was on 10 Jan 1946 at Camp Evans, Fort Monmouth, NJ (Project Diana). “The signals took 2.5 seconds to travel to the Moon and back to the Earth.” [S9, Citation]
- Diana's set-up (secondary, S16): an 8×8 dipole array, gain 24 dB, beamwidth ≈15°, driven by a 50 kW SCR-271 set; it “could be rotated in azimuth only so the experiment could only be done at moonrise and moonset”, and the team had “a limited window of opportunity of only 40 minutes each day”. A natural example of the shared-horizon-window constraint.
- Eliot's own date is 1946 in the original: “emerged in 1946, when scientists detected for the first time a man-made signal reflected from the moon.” [S1a pp. 59–60] The CIA retyped web copy says 1948 in error [S1b].
- Eliot on experiments: “The early experiments were monostatic; i.e., the transmitter and receiver were at the same location. Then bistatic experiments were performed, showing that transmitter and receiver could be separated by hundreds of miles on the earth's surface, the only requirement being that the moon be simultaneously in view of both.” [S1a p. 60]
- The Navy (NRL Radio Countermeasures Branch) began targeting Soviet radar via the Moon early. “in June 1948, after becoming aware of a study on the possibility of using the moon as a communications relay, Trexler shifted his focus to capturing radar signals reflected from the moon.” Project PAMOR (PAssive MOon Relay) was created and funded in 1950. [S6 p. 12] Trexler and Howard Lorenzen are named. Peer-reviewed confirmation: PAMOR “built radio antennae for capturing Soviet radar signals reflected from the moon's surface.” [S7 abstract]
- PAMOR's antenna was a mesh-lined parabolic hole in the ground near Stump Neck, Maryland, whose “elliptical opening … was 220 ft by 263 ft” (the full sentence reads “The elliptical opening of the antenna was 220 ft by 263 ft.”). “The first radar contact with the moon was initiated in October 1951.” [S6 pp. 12–13]
- PAMOR was not enough: “By 1954, however, the Stump Neck antenna had proven itself too weak to consistently and reliably collect Soviet radar signals.” A 600-ft steerable dish at Sugar Grove, WV was approved. [S6 p. 13] It was cancelled in 1962 “after only its turntable and pintle bearing had been completed” because satellites “could intercept emanations from the Soviet Union more effectively”. Radio astronomy was “the cover story for the project”, and “The NRL historian officially revealed the true story of the radio telescope in 2001.” [S6 pp. 13–14; S7 abstract]
- Eliot on the unbuilt dish: “The 600-foot dish that was planned for the Navy's Sugar Grove facility but never built would have been so used extensively.” [S1a p. 60]

### B3. Who ran the Hen House intercepts, where, and when
- **Target.** The “research radar we call ‘Hen House’ at the Sary Shagan missile test range in the south-central part of the Soviet Union.” [S1a p. 61]
- **Discovery of the antenna.** “During its last Soviet operations in 1960, the U-2 photographic collection system had noted a very large antenna structure near the Sary Shagan missile test range.” [S1a p. 63]
- **First signal heard (not via the Moon).** “Its signals were first heard by Western observers in 1962, not via Moon Bounce but by reflection from the ionized cloud of a Soviet atomic test explosion.” [S1a p. 63]
- **Navy first.** “The first searching via Moon Bounce was done by the Navy using its 150-foot dish on the Chesapeake. The faint signal could not be found, however, until some specific information on its frequency reached us from other sources. Navy made the first successful intercept in January 1964 and in subsequent monitoring defined roughly the parameters for the Hen House frequency and scan.” [S1a pp. 63–64]
- **CIA second.** “Stanford's 150-foot dish at Palo Alto, because of its potential with respect to eastward-looking Soviet radars, was chosen for CIA's Moon Bounce collection project. Quite sophisticated collection equipment, including two unique receivers, was built especially for this purpose and installed there. In August 1965 the Palo Alto project made its first intercept of the Hen House radar, which remains its most important target.” [S1a p. 64]
- **Analysis effort.** “CIA has undertaken a continuing analysis effort … using data from the Department of Defense intercepts and more recently from Palo Alto.” [S1a p. 64]
- **Receiving antennas named by Eliot.** “the ones at the Grand Bahama tracking station, the 150-foot dishes at the Naval Research Laboratory's Chesapeake Bay Annex and at Stanford University, and the large dish at Sugar Grove, West Virginia.” [S1a p. 60] No diameter is given for Grand Bahama or Sugar Grove.
- **Antennas were scarce.** “Very large receiving antennas are necessary to capture enough energy from this weak signal to hear it and distinguish it from other signals. Most of the very large steerable antennas in the Western world have been put to work on Moon Bounce intercepts.” [S1a p. 60]
- **Dish size rule.** “A 150-foot dish is about the minimum size usable on radars of normal power, so very few are available for our purpose.” [S1a p. 60]
- **Duration.** No end date appears in any source found. The documented span is 1948 (NRL concept) to the 1967 article, which describes the effort as continuing (“continuing analysis effort”, Palo Alto's “most important target”). Do not say how or when it ended.
- **The Stanford Dish.** It is a 150-ft antenna installed in late Sept 1961, “designed and built by Stanford Research Institute under a program supported by the Air Force Office of Aerospace Research and the Defense Atomic Support Agency”, cost $350,000, with a 20–55 MHz transmitter. [S13 Physics Today 1961] SRI says “the Dish is owned by the federal government and operated by SRI”, that the government funded SRI “to the tune of $350,000”, and that “The Dish performed its reconnaissance mission admirably” by listening to ionospheric signals from nuclear-test explosions [S13 SRI 2024]. An SRI engineer later said, “We were … listening to signals from [the Soviet Union] as they bounced off of the moon, using the moon as a reflector,” and “The Dish was basically a giant spying device” (reporter's words). [S13 KQED] These two statements are not contemporaneous documents.

### B4. Other US moon-intercept efforts (the video should not merge these with B3)
- **NSA at Arecibo (Gerson).** Gerson (NSA) did the 1957 calculation that Moon reflection was feasible: “Late one summer night in 1957, Eugene Fergusson of RADE and I examined the possibility of receiving signals returned to earth from orbiting reflectors. We then extended the calculations to include reflections from the moon, and as an afterthought, from Mars and Venus. We were both somewhat surprised with the results: the concept was feasible if a sufficiently high gain antenna were available.” [S2 p. 47]
  - “I wanted to use AIO [Arecibo Ionospheric Observatory] for NSA purposes because it provided the high gain that would allow intercept via moonbounce.” [S2 p. 47]
  - “William Scheerer of the Army Security Agency and I examined the potential in detail, and published ‘Moonbounce Potential from Scooped [?] Antennas’ in three volumes. It showed that high power earthbound transmitters could be received at AIO. (We considered only vhf and uhf.)” [S2 p. 47] The title is underlined in the scan and its fourth word is hard to read (probably “Scooped”, matching Gerson's later “scooped antenna”).
  - ARPA's initial resistance: Herzfeld said AIO “had been funded as a wholly scientific and open facility, would not be allowed to undertake classified studies”. “He later relented.” “The open designation of our work was a study of lunar temperatures.” [S2 p. 47]
  - Funding: “Mitford Mathews, Deputy Director for Research, provided us with $30K to undertake the experiment.” Joseph Norvell, Earnest Marsh and John Peasley designed the equipment. [S2 p. 47]
  - **Result:** “We were fortunate. After just about one week of operation we intercepted Soviet radar operating on the Arctic coast.” [S2 p. 48] The date is not given. The article's chronology is loose (it says AIO funding began “during the early 1950s”, though ARPA was created in 1958).
  - Scrapped idea: ARPA offered to construct “a scooped antenna for NSA, in the Seychelles or elsewhere. A nuclear detonation would be employed … We never pursued this possibility.” [S2 p. 48]
  - Gerson's other quote: “What about an intercept site on the moon? It is conceivable.” [S2 p. 48]
  - Richelson independently states that NSA used Arecibo to intercept “signals that had originally been transmitted from a Soviet Arctic Coast radar”. [S8, citing Wizards of Langley pp. 89–90]
  - The NSA Technical Journal's “Extending the Radio Horizon for Intercept Purposes by Using the Moon as a Reflector” appeared in vol. X no. 2, Spring 1965 (classification C). Companion item: “Broadband Planar Log-Periodic Feed for the Spherical Reflector Used at Arecibo Ionospheric Laboratory,” vol. X no. 3, Summer 1965. In the index both entries sit in the same redacted-author block. [S3 index p. 91] The authorship link is **not stated**.
- **Air Force and others.** “The Air Force also had its own moonbounce project — designated FLOWER GARDEN — which relied on several antennas, including the 250-foot antenna at Jodrell Bank Radio Observatory in England.” [S8, citing Richelson] Secondary only. I could not check the book.
- **CIA Tall King mapping (Poteat memoir).** “Melody's success with the high-powered, missile-related radar led to the idea of using the moon as a distant bi-static reflector to intercept and locate the Tall King radar systems deployed in the Soviet Union.” “sensitive ELINT receivers, tuned to the Tall King frequency, were attached to the giant 60-foot RCA radar antenna just off the New Jersey Turnpike near Moorestown and pointed at the moon.” “The ELINT receivers were also optimized for the effects of the moon as a reflector, that is, using the lab's ‘matched filter’ techniques. Over time, as the Earth and moon revolved and rotated, all the Soviet radar sites came into view one at a time, and their precise geographic locations were plotted.” [S4 p. 23; same text S5 pp. 53–54] Poteat's motive was Oxcart planning: Tall King was “the Oxcart's nemesis” [S4 p. 23]. This is a single-source memoir; the programme is undated and has no quantified results.
- **Navy Communication Moon Relay (not ELINT).** It was a civilian-looking comms link between Annapolis/Cheltenham and Hawaii (84-ft dishes), inaugurated Jan 1960. “The principal operational disadvantage of the CMR was simply the availability of the moon, which had to be within sight of both of the link terminals.” Ground stations were manned “from moonrise in Hawaii to moonset in Maryland”. [S6 p. 23] It is a clean real-world illustration of the shared-visibility window. The CMR antennas were reused for TRSSCOM ship communications. [S6 pp. 46–47]

### B5. What was learned (Hen House) — all Eliot unless noted
- “Three major discoveries have been made about the signal.” [p. 64]
  1. **Spread-spectrum mode.** “the signal has a ‘spread-spectrum’ mode. This means that the spectrum (frequency spread) of the signal can be intentionally broadened to increase either the radar's range or its range-rate resolution, that is its accuracy in reading its target's speed.” [pp. 64–65]
  2. **Advanced scanning.** “We expected to see a regular scanning, or ‘search’ mode, and a tracking mode … Both of these have been observed. In the latter, the Soviets, apparently just for practice, have set the radar to track the moon for as much as half an hour. This makes the intercept job much easier, as we then see the signal continuously rather than in short bursts as the beam swings by the moon.” [p. 65] There was also “a combination mode wherein it is basically scanning but will dwell for a short time on any target it sees … Since the scan and dwell times are quite short, it must operate under computer control.” [p. 65] (“We imagine the radar can keep track of several targets at once” is flagged as inference.)
  3. **Power.** “The most recent significant observation is that the transmitter's peak power is about 25 megawatts. This, if correct, makes it one of the highest-powered radars in the world.” [p. 65]
- **Conclusion.** “All of these observations lead us to believe that the Hen House is a new, sophisticated ABM radar. Knowledge of Soviet ABM capability has become of increasingly critical importance to the United States. By accurately defining the parameters of the new radar, we can now start the design of countermeasures and tactics to reduce its effectiveness.” [p. 65]
- **Verdict.** “The Moon Bounce effort is one of those intelligence collection techniques which seemed at first ‘far out’ but has in the event more than paid for itself.” [p. 65]
- **Why research radars.** “Research radars are the most important to intercept, since they give hints about what may be operational in the future.” [p. 63]
- **Hen House frequency.** Eliot gives no frequency. Poteat says only that it “was known to be in the VHF band” [S4 p. 26].

### B6. Quantities stated by Eliot
- **Signal strength.** “A typical signal received via Moon Bounce is more than a million billion times weaker than if it were received in an airplane ten miles from the transmitter.” [p. 60]
- **Pulse smear if the Moon were rough.** “about .0116 second”, versus radar pulses “on the order of .00001 second long”. [pp. 60–61]
- **Real Moon.** “the moon appears only slightly rough to radio waves; most of the reflected energy comes back from an area at the near point just a few miles in diameter … The moon reflection we get appears to come from a few large humps, not a uniformly rough surface. Depending upon their relative positions, these individual facets may give reflections that add or subtract from one another, creating a larger signal back on earth or making it even disappear.” [p. 61]
- **Figure 1 (p. 62).** “Oscillograph traces of three consecutive pulses spaced 20 seconds apart. Each transmitted pulse was 800 microseconds long.” The traces show lengthening, a degraded rise time and a near-original shape. Eliot does not say which radar this was.
- **Intercept hours per month at Palo Alto** (Eliot's estimates, assuming the radar is on):
  - 360° rotating beam: “about 38 hours a month”, “somewhat longer” at Chesapeake [p. 61]
  - westward-looking only: “about 20 hours” [p. 63]
  - actual 31.9° sector: “limiting our possible intercept time there to 18 hours” [p. 63]
  - “In practice, we unfortunately have somewhat less time than that … it may not be operating … Being a research device, it can be expected to be ‘down’ a large portion of the time. The Soviets might even avoid letting its beam hit the moon.” [p. 63]
- **Beam geometry.** Long-range radars “sweep over a sector of azimuth keeping very close to the horizon”; “very little energy transmitted above 20 degrees or so”. [pp. 61, 63]
- **Why Palo Alto beat the East Coast for this use.** “what advantage the eastern location has in intercept time is offset by the advantage of the Palo Alto location in being able to see eastward-looking Soviet radars which are nearly hidden from the eastern United States.” [p. 63]

## C. Mechanics, step by step (for the diagram)

Cited steps are Eliot (S1a) unless marked. “[computed]” means derived by me; see B7.

1. **The radar beam.** A Soviet long-range search or ABM radar (Hen House, Sary Shagan) sweeps a narrow sector in azimuth, “very close to the horizon”. Its sector is 31.9° wide. Almost nothing goes above ~20°. Most of the beam goes out over the horizon into space, because the wavelength is too short for the ionosphere to bend it back [pp. 59, 61, 63].
2. **The first condition: the beam hits the Moon.** Only when the Moon crosses the sweep sector, “within a few degrees of the radar's horizon”, does any energy strike it [p. 61]. Each sweep gives a flash of signal; if the radar tracks the Moon, the flash becomes a continuous signal [pp. 61, 65].
3. **Out-leg.** The pulse takes ≈1.28 s to reach the Moon [computed from 384,400 km].
4. **The Moon scatters.** It is not a mirror. For radio it is “only slightly rough”, so most returned energy comes from a few-mile-wide patch at the sub-Earth point, plus “a few large humps”. Facets add and cancel, so pulse amplitude and length vary pulse to pulse (Fig. 1, p. 62). If it were fully rough, each pulse would smear to 11.6 ms [pp. 60–61; computed 2R/c].
5. **Back-leg and weakness.** The echo takes another ≈1.28 s and arrives ≈2.4–2.7 s after the pulse was sent [computed; S9 gives 2.5 s]. Only a tiny fraction survives: >10¹⁵ times weaker than at ten miles [p. 60]. A dish of ≥150 ft is needed [p. 60].
6. **The second condition: the Moon is up at the receiver.** The same Moon must be “simultaneously visible at our receiving site” [pp. 59–61]. A site 95° of arc from Sary Shagan can see the Moon at the radar's horizon while the Moon is also above its own horizon [computed]. The practical window is the overlap of the two sites' Moon-above-horizon periods. The Navy's CMR link described exactly this: usable “from moonrise in Hawaii to moonset in Maryland” [S6 p. 23].
7. **Choosing the site.** Palo Alto (Stanford 150-ft dish) was chosen for CIA because eastward-looking radars are “nearly hidden from the eastern United States”. The Navy's Chesapeake dish gets a little more time for a 360° sweep but loses that eastward advantage [p. 63].
8. **Finding the signal.** The signal was too faint to find until “specific information on its frequency reached us from other sources” [p. 63]. Detection used special receivers (CIA's “two unique receivers”, p. 64). Poteat adds receivers tuned to the target frequency and **“matched filter”** processing for the Moon's effect [S4 p. 23]. No source describes Doppler processing.
9. **Parameter extraction.** Analysts measured scan mode, dwell behaviour, spectrum spreading and peak power [pp. 64–65].
10. **Geolocation idea (Gerson).** Gerson noticed that “the location of the target on the earth also could be determined by noting the time when the received signal abruptly ‘died.’ If the transmitter was still emitting, this loss of signal meant that the transmitter must lie on a certain line of position determined only by moon-earth geometry. Observations made on the same transmitter over a period of weeks then would provide additional lines of position (because of changing earth-moon geometry).” [S2 p. 47]. In geometric terms [my interpretation], when the Moon sets at the transmitter's horizon the echo vanishes, so the transmitter lies on a circle of points having the Moon on the horizon at that instant; repeated nights give crossing circles (a fix). Poteat's version: “all the Soviet radar sites came into view one at a time, and their precise geographic locations were plotted” [S4 p. 23].
11. **Limits.** Intercept hours per month are small (≤38 h even for a full sweep; 18 h for the actual sector, less in practice); the radar may be off, or may avoid the Moon [p. 63]; the echo is distorted (Fig. 1) but “still contains much useful information” [p. 61]; very few dishes (≥150 ft) exist [p. 60].

## D. Uncertain, contested, or to avoid saying

1. **Do not call the source "NSA Technical Journal".** Eliot and Gerson are both CIA *Studies in Intelligence* (see section 0).
2. **Do not use "1948" for the first Moon echo** (CIA retyped-PDF error). Use 10 Jan 1946 (S9; S1a). 1948 is correct only for the *Navy's decision to target Soviet radar via the Moon* (S6 p. 12).
3. **"The CIA started collecting in 1964" (S17) is wrong.** The Navy's first success was Jan 1964; CIA's first Palo Alto intercept was Aug 1965 (S1a pp. 63–64). S17 also says the CIA waited for “the Soviets to scan an object in front of the moon”, which is not in any source. The beam must hit the Moon itself.
4. **S17's "it could have negated the American nuclear deterrent" is unsupported.** Eliot says only that Hen House is “a new, sophisticated ABM radar” and that countermeasures and tactics could now be designed.
5. **25 MW is an estimate.** Eliot: “about 25 megawatts. This, if correct…” Wikipedia's Dnestr article (S15) gives 1.25 MW per transmitter for the production radar and 154–162 MHz, a 0.8 ms pulse, and a Sary Shagan prototype that first detected an object on 17 Sep 1961. I did not reconcile these. Say "estimated about 25 megawatts" and do not use the Wikipedia figures. Eliot's Fig. 1 pulse length (800 µs) matches 0.8 ms, but Eliot never says Fig. 1 is Hen House, so do not claim it.
6. **"Hen House" is NATO's name** and was later applied to a family of production early-warning radars (Dnestr/Dnepr). Eliot's Hen House is the Sary Shagan research radar. Do not mix in the later sites.
7. **Poteat contradicts the idea that the Moon was how Hen House was first detected.** He says his Briar Patch tropospheric-scatter station (a Baltic island) “picked up the very first Hen House transmission” [S4 p. 26]. Eliot says first heard 1962 via a nuclear-test ionised cloud, and the first Moon intercept was Jan 1964 by the Navy. These conflict or concern different stages; do not say "the Moon was the first detection". Also, Poteat's MELODY (Iran, reflections off Soviet missiles) is not a moonbounce programme.
8. **"Nearly antipodal."** Eliot writes “the nearly antipodal Sary Shagan” [p. 63]. By great-circle distance it is only ≈95° from Palo Alto [computed]; the sites are ≈164° apart in longitude. Avoid "antipodal".
9. **Poteat/Bamford Tall King moon programme.** It is single-source, undated and unquantified. Attribute it ("a former CIA engineer recalled…"). Bamford's blurb is an echo of Poteat, not corroboration.
10. **Gerson/Arecibo timeline.** Undated in the article. Its chronology is loose (ARPA “early 1950s”). The only timing claim is “about one week of operation”. Do not date the intercept; do not claim it was Hen House (it was “Soviet radar operating on the Arctic coast”).
11. **Do not say how the signal was distinguished by Doppler or pulse parameters.** No source says this. What is sourced: prior frequency information from other sources (Eliot), matched filtering (Poteat), and loss-of-signal timing for location (Gerson). K1JT discusses Moon libration Doppler spread (a limb-to-limb spread up to ~30 Hz per GHz), but it does not appear in Eliot; use only as explanatory physics, labelled as such.
12. **Duration and end of the programme.** Not documented. Do not say "it ran until X" or "was cancelled". The only cancellation documented is the unbuilt 600-ft Sugar Grove dish (1962), driven by cost and by ELINT satellites [S6 pp. 13–14].
13. **Sugar Grove.** Eliot lists “the large dish at Sugar Grove” among antennas used. The 600-ft telescope never became a collector. The smaller Sugar Grove instrument was later used to receive ELINT-ship data, which is a different function [S6 p. 13].
14. **Do not say the Moon works "like a mirror" or that Moon phase matters.** Eliot says it is a rough-ish scatterer. Illumination is irrelevant to radio echoes. What matters is that the Moon is above both horizons and inside the beam (my physics reasoning, not an Eliot statement).
15. **Soviet awareness.** Unknown. Eliot only speculates they “might even avoid letting its beam hit the moon”, while also saying they tracked it “apparently just for practice”.
16. **Wire-service-style claims to avoid.** "Spy ship moonbounce dish": Bamford quotes one former crewman calling it “more cover story than anything else” [S14]. "Kagnew Station moonbounce dishes": Bamford calls them only “planned”. Neither is established as an operating intercept.
17. **Project Diana's eavesdropping.** S16 says Pentagon interest in eavesdropping on the Soviet Union “ultimately proved unsuccessful”; S6 says the Army “concluded … that nothing of military use would come of this work”. Both refer to the 1946 Army work, not the later Navy/CIA/NSA programmes.
18. **Unseen documents.** I found no NRO or CIA CREST document specific to Moon-bounce beyond Eliot's article (the CIA reading room was down for maintenance during research). The NSATJ 1965 article text and Richelson's pp. 89–90 were not seen.
