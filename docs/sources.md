# THE MOON FILES — sources and fact-check

Every narrated claim and on-screen figure, mapped to its source. Primary sources are declassified CIA *Studies in Intelligence* articles (US Government works, public domain). Page numbers refer to the original journal pagination where known.

## Primary sources

| Key | Citation | Copy used |
|---|---|---|

## Narration, line by line

| # | Narration | Support |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | In January 1964, the U.S. Navy intercepted a secret Soviet radar — by listening to the Moon. | ELIOT pp. 63–64: "Navy made the first successful intercept in January 1964" (Hen House, via Moon Bounce, using the 150-ft Chesapeake dish). |
| 2 | A U-2 had photographed its giant antenna at Sary Shagan, a Soviet missile range. | ELIOT: "During its last Soviet operations in 1960, the U-2 photographic collection system had noted a very large antenna structure near the Sary Shagan missile test range." The on-screen plan view is labelled SCHEMATIC; it is not a U-2 photograph. |
| 3 | But radar waves travel in straight lines. From deep inside the USSR, they skim past the horizon into space. | ELIOT p. 59: radar wavelengths "pass through [the ionosphere] and are generally lost in outer space… we are unable to observe them from ground collection sites when they are more than a few hundred miles inside the denied area." |
| 4 | In 1962, it was heard briefly, off the ionized cloud of a Soviet nuclear test. | ELIOT p. 63: "Its signals were first heard by Western observers in 1962, not via Moon Bounce but by reflection from the ionized cloud of a Soviet atomic test explosion… this brief, crude intercept." (Poteat claims an earlier Briar Patch troposcatter detection, so the film does not say "first".) |
| 5 | They needed a steadier reflector. The Moon. | ELIOT, "New Reflector": the Moon as a passive reflector; bistatic Moon Bounce "the only requirement being that the moon be simultaneously in view of both". The script says "reflector", not "mirror", because the Moon scatters rather than reflecting specularly. |
| 6 | When it sat low on the radar's horizon, and above America too, a sliver of each pulse bounced back down. | ELIOT p. 61: the Moon "must be passing through the radar's search sector, be within a few degrees of the radar's horizon, and be visible to us"; most energy "is reflected out into space and never returns to earth." |
| 7 | The echo was over a million billion times weaker than ten miles from the radar. | ELIOT p. 60: "more than a million billion times weaker than if it were received in an airplane ten miles from the transmitter." |
| 8 | Only 150-foot dishes could hear it. | ELIOT p. 60: "A 150-foot dish is about the minimum size usable on radars of normal power." On screen: "ABOUT THE MINIMUM SIZE USABLE". |
| 9 | The Navy's dish on Chesapeake Bay got it first; the CIA's, at Stanford, in 1965. | ELIOT pp. 63–64: Navy 150-ft dish on the Chesapeake, January 1964; Stanford's 150-ft dish at Palo Alto chosen for CIA's collection project, first Hen House intercept August 1965. |
| 10 | Geometry allowed about 18 hours a month. | ELIOT p. 63: Hen House sweeps "an azimuth sector of only 31.9 degrees, limiting our possible intercept time there [Palo Alto] to 18 hours" (per month). On screen: "AT BEST — IF THE RADAR WAS ON" (the radar "may not be operating"). The pattern of lit cells is illustrative; only the total of 18 is sourced. |
| 11 | It was enough: computer-controlled scanning, a spread-spectrum mode, about 25 megawatts of peak power. A new anti-missile radar. | ELIOT pp. 64–65: "spread-spectrum" mode; scan, track and scan-and-dwell modes, "it must operate under computer control"; "peak power is about 25 megawatts. This, if correct…" (shown as EST.); "a new, sophisticated ABM radar." |
| 12 | Soviet operators even locked it onto the Moon — for practice. | ELIOT p. 65: "the Soviets, apparently just for practice, have set the radar to track the moon for as much as half an hour." On screen: "UP TO 30 MIN". |
| 13 | Years earlier, a touring Soviet exhibition showed off a Lunik: the final stage of a Moon rocket. Surely, a mock-up. | FINER p. 33: an exhibition "of its industrial and economic achievements" toured several countries; "the last-stage Lunik space vehicle… It was presumably a mock-up." SHELNOV dates the Mexico City showing to 21 Nov – 15 Dec 1959, which is before the 1964 intercept. |
| 14 | It wasn't. U.S. intelligence officers spent a day with it: a real production vehicle, engine removed. | FINER pp. 33–34: "a group of intelligence officers had unrestricted access to the Lunik for some 24 hours… a production item from which the engine and most electrical and electronic components had been removed." |
| 15 | That data helped the CIA measure the Soviet ICBM: 18,000 pounds thrown 3,800 miles. | W&G pp. 9–10: the stage weighed "about 2,600 pounds dry… 18,000 pounds with the propellant tanks filled and the payload on board… the ICBM had a capability of throwing 18,000 pounds a distance of 3,800 miles." The link to the exhibition is FINER footnote 1. The film says "helped" because the estimate also used telemetry and radar tracking. |
| 16 | Later, for its factory markings, the CIA borrowed it. Overnight. | FINER p. 34: markings had not been copied precisely enough, so another access was sought "for a factory markings team"; "the second time by borrowing it overnight and returning it before the Soviets missed it." |
| 17 | It left as the day's last truck. At the final turnoff, a new driver took the wheel. The real one spent the night in a hotel. | FINER p. 36: "the last truckload of the day"; Station cars ahead and behind; "stopped at the last possible turn-off, a canvas was thrown over the crate, and a new driver took over. | 18 | In a rented salvage yard, four specialists opened the roof, unbolted the base, and slipped inside, shoes off. | FINER pp. 35–37: "four-man team of specialists from the joint Factory Markings Center"; "a salvage yard which had been rented"; roof of "2-inch tongue-and-groove planks nailed down with 5-inch spikes"; base cap "attached to its flange by some 130 square-headed bolts"; nose team "took off their shoes so as to leave no telltale scars." |
| 19 | By 7 a.m., the truck was at the rail yard. The Soviet checker showed no surprise. | FINER pp. 38–39: "came to the yard at 7:00 a.m. and found the truck with the Lunik awaiting him. He showed no surprise." |
| 20 | The markings revealed the likely manufacturer, and that it was the fifth one built. | FINER p. 39: "probable identification of the producer of this Lunik stage, the fact that it was the fifth one produced, identification of three electrical producers." |

## On-screen values that are computed or illustrative

- **384,400 km, ≈1.3 s each way.** Mean Earth–Moon distance (standard value); 384,400 km ÷ 299,792 km/s = 1.28 s. Computed, not from Eliot. The cold open times the pulse at 1.28 s up and 1.28 s down.
- **Earth–Moon geometry diagram.** The Moon is placed over the North Pacific (30°N, 150°W). From there it is ≈94° from Sary Shagan, on the radar's horizon, and ≈24° from Stanford, high in the sky. Computed for illustration and labelled NOT TO SCALE. The ±16° beam sweep reflects Eliot's 31.9° sector.
- **Lunik drawings.** Redrawn from W&G Figure 1 (17′6″ long, 8′5″ diameter; payload orb, antenna probe, four tank sections, engine bay). The three nose windows come from FINER. The painted exhibition finish, pedestal and visitors are illustrative.
- **Truck-run map.** Schematic, labelled as such. It follows the sequence FINER describes; streets and distances are invented.
- **Salvage-yard cut-away.** The crate's 20 ft length and 14 ft peak (FINER) set the scale; the wall height is inferred. Timings of 7:30 p.m., 4:00 a.m. and 7:00 a.m. are FINER's.
- **Factory markings.** Shown redacted, because the CIA articles redact them.
- **Sary Shagan pin.** Placed at the test-range headquarters (≈46.1°N, 73.6°E). The radar's exact position is not given in the sources.

## Things deliberately not said

The film does not:
- name the city, country, people, factory or Luna mission for the Lunik (location appears only as an attributed footnote);
- call the Soviet checker KGB, or refer to guards or weapons;
- claim the Moon intercepts were how Hen House was *first* detected;
- name a Doppler or pulse-identification method;
- give an end date for the programme;
- claim the Lunik operation shaped NASA timelines.

These claims appear in popular accounts but are not supported by the primary documents. See `docs/research/moonbounce.md` §D and the Lunik research notes.
